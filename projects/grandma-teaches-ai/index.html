01

What it is

Grandma Teaches AI is a young project about everyday artificial intelligence (AI). It is built around a painted, AI-voiced character: a painted gouache illustration with a generated voice, not a real person. The character explains everyday AI in short videos, in plain words, with curiosity instead of fear.

The character’s writing and generated voice are meant to sound like a sharp, kind teacher at a kitchen table: real examples, a little dry humor, and language you do not need a technical background to follow. The writing, the voice, and the pictures are made with AI tools, and that is disclosed, not hidden. A small credit, “created by FringeNode,” stays on the work.

This is the first FringeNode project that feels ready to show, and it is still early. The first episodes are already made. They cover basics such as how a model “watches” a video, where a chatbot’s answer comes from, words people hear but rarely can explain, why models make things up, and how to recognize some common scams, including a voice that sounds like someone you love. A public home for the videos is coming soon.

02

How it came to be

The project started from a simple wish: explain artificial intelligence in a clear, steady voice, not like a lecture and not like a warning siren. Around this painted character we built a repeatable way of working, because a character only holds if the care is the same every time.

A topic is researched and sourced first. A script is written in the character’s voice, and a person reviews it before any audio is generated. A look is chosen for that episode — light, color, mood — and held from the first frame to the last. An AI voice is generated from the script, pictures are painted to match the lines, and every frame is checked against a written quality list: Does the picture match the line? Is the type readable on a phone? Did we lean on vague comparisons? Is a real person’s face or name in the shot?

A second, stricter gate runs before anyone is asked to publish. It blocks real people in names, faces, or lookalikes. It flags wording that could be mistaken for a scam or a get-rich pitch. It checks that titles are honest and that the AI-made voice and painted character are clearly disclosed. Nothing goes out without an explicit yes in words.

03

Skills gained

  • Context engineering
  • Multi-agent orchestration
  • Quality assurance (QA) checklists
  • Script writing
  • Voice production
  • Visual consistency
  • Safety review gates
  • Fact-checking
  • Context engineering: shaping the work with instructions, examples, memory, and tools, instead of retraining a model from scratch — here, holding a stable painted, AI‑voiced character, a house style, and a set of hard rules across many episodes.
  • Multi-agent orchestration: splitting the job among specialists that hand work to each other: research and scripting, picture and voice production, an automatic quality check, and a final safety review.
  • Quality assurance (QA) checklists: written lists every episode is checked against, covering questions like whether the picture matches the line, whether the type is readable on a phone, and whether a real person’s face or name is in the shot.
  • Script writing: writing each episode in the painted character’s voice, then having a person review the script before any audio is generated.
  • Voice production: generating an AI voice from the reviewed script, and clearly disclosing that the voice is AI-made.
  • Visual consistency: choosing a look for each episode — light, color, and mood — and holding it from the first frame to the last.
  • Safety review gates: a written checklist every video has to pass before anyone can ask to post it.
  • Fact-checking: researching and sourcing a topic first, before a script is written.

04

What it can do

Short, clear videos can reach people who will never read a long study guide, including people who have been told AI is either magic or a threat. For families, that can mean a shared language for a confusing topic. For older viewers, it can mean recognizing a voice-clone call or a common scam before money or trust is lost.

We cannot promise that a video will stop a crime. We can promise to teach the tells plainly, to keep the warning from sounding like an offer, and to put curiosity ahead of panic.

The honest hope is modest: more people who can tell a tool from a mind, and who stay rooted in ordinary judgment — including care for one another, and for the living world that all this computing draws power from.
