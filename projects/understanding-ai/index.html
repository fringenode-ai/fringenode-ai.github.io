Understanding AI

A beginner field guide to open-weight models, running them locally, agents, and privacy.

Read the guide

01

What it is

Field Guide 01 is a free beginner study guide to what an artificial intelligence (AI) model actually is, how to run one on your own computer, and how to think about agents and privacy.

It walks the whole picture in order, in 13 sections, in about a 19-minute read. It starts from the basics: a model is a large file of learned numbers, plus the software that runs it. From there it covers open-weight and closed models, who makes them, how to compare them, how to run one locally, how to keep that setup private, how agents are shaped, and where the field is heading. Each section ends with key terms in plain words, and a 22-question self-check waits at the bottom.

The guide has its own look, separate from the rest of this site: a dark, below-the-waterline design you descend as you read. It is labeled as a late-2026 snapshot, because model names and rankings change quickly, and it asks readers to check official sources before relying on a detail. You can read it now at fringenode-ai.github.io/understanding-ai.

02

How it came to be

The guide began as a way to learn the field in public, without assuming the reader already speaks the jargon. The writing follows a simple rule: start from what a model is, then let each part rest on the one before. Only after the foundations are in place does it move on to makers, home setups, privacy, agents, and work in the field.

It was built as a static web page — ordinary HyperText Markup Language (HTML) and Cascading Style Sheets (CSS), no app behind it — so it can be read in a browser or saved as a file. The visual world was a deliberate choice. The topics include power, data centers, privacy, and who is building what, so the page was designed to feel like a field notebook you take seriously, not a marketing site.

Factual claims were checked against official or reputable sources. The last sections look at the physical side of the industry, including why large data centers are being built and why electric power has become a bottleneck, and they separate what is known from what is still speculation. A self-check at the end lets a reader test what they actually held onto.

03

Skills gained

  • Plain-language technical writing
  • Research and source-checking
  • Information design
  • Static site building
  • Mobile-friendly layout
  • Self-check quizzes

Making the guide meant turning a dense field into a path a newcomer can follow. That included defining jargon at first use, building tables and term cards that still work on a phone, keeping a consistent voice across 13 sections, and dating the snapshot so the page stays honest as the field moves. Building a long, sourced guide this way is also practice in the same ideas the later sections teach: gathering the right material, shaping a clear brief, and giving a reader everything they need in the right order.

04

What it can do

The guide is for people who want to understand the systems they already use, and for people who want to run a model at home so their chats do not have to leave the house. That is a practical kind of privacy, and a way to learn by doing.

It can also change how someone sees the physical side of AI. A chat window feels light, but behind it are buildings full of computers and a growing demand for electric power. Seeing that clearly is part of using these tools with care — for people, and for the living world that all that computing depends on.

It will not make anyone an engineer by itself. It will give a solid map, a set of terms that mean something, and a habit of checking official sources as the field moves.
