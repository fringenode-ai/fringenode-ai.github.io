Checkpoint 02 · The map of parts

Land Restoration Tech Tree

What already exists, what still has to be built, and what must exist first, for a local computer that helps land recover.

Checkpoint 02 of 05 · November 26, 2026

This post is the map of parts for a FringeNode site: what already exists, what still has to be built, and what must exist first, for a small computer that stays on a piece of land and helps that land recover.

It is the second of five checkpoint posts. The first, The Closed Loop, explained the idea as one cycle: learn from healthy land, watch a damaged plot, make one small fix, measure it, and repeat. This post lays out the parts of that cycle. You can open each part to see what it depends on, what it unlocks, and how proven the earlier work is.

The land gets a brain and hands of its own, run by the people who live there.

The closed loop, in brief

A node is a small, solar-powered computer with sensors, mounted on the land. It is an edge computer: the computing happens on site, not in a distant data center. It is meant to keep working without an internet connection.

The new invention is the closed loop: a local, community-owned node that learns from healthy land, designs the plot, and later runs friendly earth-construction machines under hard safety rules, while a record on that node gets more useful over decades.

Many of the pieces already exist as separate products or research programs. FringeNode’s own scan of existing work did not find one system that does all of this on a single plot, offline-first, owned by the people who live there. That combination is the goal. It is not built yet.

A node’s record is practice and learning. It is not an official wetland delineation (the legal mapping of a wetland’s boundary). Delineation is done by qualified professionals using field indicators of plants, soils, and water.

Early, mid, and late game

The path is written backward from the late-game picture, then the mid-game offer, then the first node. That method is called backcasting: each earlier step is chosen because it leads to the end goal.

Now. A written plan, this map, and a first node being assembled on a workbench (the bench node). There is no field data yet.

Early game. One sensing node at a private test site starts an honest local record of water, soil, and weather. People do the small fixes. A person approves every change. Safety is practiced as habit from the first day: the node works with no uplink, outbound records can leave on a walk-out drive, signed updates arrive by hand, and a physical power cut is mocked up before any tool moves dirt. The point of year one is not to outrun today’s forest sensors. It is to have compute on the land that someone nearby can keep, and a record that has already begun to lengthen.

Mid game. That same kind of record becomes a noded wetland: year-round, on-site sensing of water, soil, plants, and quality, stored on the local node, meant to help the professionals who already monitor wetlands. Software may draft a monitoring report; a qualified person still checks and signs it. Software never auto-files, and never pretends to be a legal delineation. Local people get first offer of paid field tasks the node helps describe. A shallow-water explorer comes later, if it earns its place. Restoration machines wait until the node, the record, and the relationships are real. People stay at the center: the node finds, describes, and checks work; trained local people do it with simple tools.

Late game. A forest of fringe nodes, each the brain of its plot, still offline-first, with no required cloud. Nodes in healthy areas learn patterns — water, soil, microtopography, and plant guilds, and later underground connections if the science holds. A design engine copies those patterns onto damaged land as plot-by-plot plans a person still signs. Simple, wireless, friendly earth-construction machines — earthmovers and dexterous planters, not humanoid or military — do some of the work, including a possible quiet electric night shift, under the full safety stack. People decide. Closest residents weigh most, less with distance. Downstream people are counted fairly, because water carries every choice. The record runs for decades: the longer it runs, the more useful it becomes.

A later research branch. Fungal networks (mycelium, the thread-like body of a fungus) as a possible future sensor. This branch sits last on the map, labeled later research. It is not part of the first node, and it is not part of the mid-game monitoring offer. Two-way “healing nudges” stay speculative.

How to read the map

The map is modeled on the 2024 National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) Technology Taxonomy: three levels, coded the same way. Level 1 is an area, for example LR01 Sensing. Level 2 is a subarea. Level 3 is a technology type, the clickable parts below. There are 35 of those parts, in eight areas.

  • Status is for FringeNode’s version of that part, not for the predecessor. Exists means a usable method or product already covers this rung; FringeNode would integrate or sit beside it, not invent it. Buildable now means it can be built or practiced with current parts and methods. Research means science, design, or field methods are still open. Speculative means it is not established, and must not be sold as fact.
  • Stage is when this part belongs on the path: early, mid, late, or later research.
  • Evidence labels apply to the cited earlier work. Field-proven means used in real projects, written into official methods, or shown in established field records. Lab demo means shown in a laboratory or as a research prototype, not as a finished field product. Vendor claim means described by the company or project that built it, not independently checked here. Claims without a primary source already on file are flagged, not filled in. No numbers or sources are invented.

The safety parts (LR03) describe the what and why only: offline-first operation, send-only outbound records, signed updates by hand, a physical power cut mocked up before anything moves dirt, and a person who approves large earthworks. They are not a wiring guide, a parts list, or a how-to.
